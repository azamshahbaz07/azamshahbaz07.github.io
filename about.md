# About Me

I got into engineering through a question I couldn't stop asking: when I play VALORANT and a shot I was sure landed doesn't register, what actually happened? Somewhere between my mouse click and the server's decision there's a client, a network, a game server running a tick loop, and a matchmaking system deciding who I'm even playing against. I wanted to understand every layer of that. That curiosity still drives most of what I build.

My path here hasn't been a straight line. I started in mechanical engineering, moved into electrical, and finally landed in computer engineering. Along the way I took coursework at Kennesaw State, Georgia Gwinnett College, and Georgia State before transferring to Georgia Tech. Each step taught me something the next one needed. Mechanical engineering taught me to respect physical constraints. Electrical engineering taught me what a signal really is. Computer engineering is where I get to work with both: software that has to respect the hardware it runs on.

## What I've worked on

My research and work experience has taken me to a few different parts of the stack. At Kennesaw State's Intelligent Biomechanics Laboratory, I implemented and compared four segmentation models, including a U-Net, to track organ lumens across more than 13,000 de-identified video frames, and built visualizations that helped Emory clinicians analyze procedures faster. At Georgia Tech's ICE Lab, I went down to the transistor level, porting analog and mixed-signal circuits from a 350 nm TSMC process to the open SKY130 PDK. Last summer at Flock Safety, I wrote Linux kernel drivers and a C++ battery-heater control daemon for Qualcomm platforms, which was my first look at what "production" really means. In the Voicu Research Lab, I built ROS 2 behavior trees for a real-time robotics pipeline and helped cut its end-to-end control latency by 25%.

On my own time, I build systems projects from scratch to learn how real infrastructure works: a persistent key-value store with crash recovery, a work-stealing thread pool with a custom allocator, and a multithreaded packet analyzer. Building them myself has taught me more than reading about them ever did.

## What I care about

Across all of this, I keep coming back to **software that has to be fast and reliable**, where "fast on average" isn't good enough and the worst case matters. That is why I'm interested in systems software, computer architecture, and low-latency engineering, and why I'm doing a BS/MS so I can go deeper in computer systems.

## Outside of class

I still play VALORANT, though now I spend as much time thinking about its netcode as I do about my aim. I also like working through math for its own sake; I've taken PDEs, complex analysis, and real analysis mostly because I enjoyed them.
